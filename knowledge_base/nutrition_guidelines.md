# Pet Nutrition Guidelines

## Dogs — Nutritional Requirements

Dogs are omnivores but thrive on high-protein diets. Look for foods where a named protein (chicken, beef, salmon) is the first ingredient.

**Macronutrient targets (dry matter basis):**
- Protein: minimum 18% for adults, 22% for puppies
- Fat: minimum 5% for adults, 8% for puppies
- Fiber: 2–5% for normal digestion

**Feeding amounts** depend on the dog's weight, activity level, and the specific food's caloric density. Follow the manufacturer's guidelines as a starting point, then adjust based on body condition score (BCS). Overfeeding is the leading cause of obesity in dogs.

**Avoid feeding dogs:**
- Chocolate (theobromine toxicity)
- Grapes and raisins (renal failure, mechanism unknown)
- Xylitol/artificial sweeteners (hypoglycemia, liver failure)
- Onions and garlic (hemolytic anemia)
- Macadamia nuts (tremors, hyperthermia)
- Raw dough (ethanol from yeast fermentation)
- Cooked bones (splintering risk)
- Alcohol and caffeine

**Treats** should not exceed 10% of daily caloric intake. Use small, low-calorie treats for training.

## Cats — Nutritional Requirements

Cats are obligate carnivores: they cannot synthesize taurine, arachidonic acid, or vitamin A from plant sources — these must come from animal protein.

**Macronutrient targets (dry matter basis):**
- Protein: minimum 26% for adults, 30% for kittens
- Fat: minimum 9%
- Taurine: minimum 0.1% (critical for heart and eye health)
- Carbohydrates: cats have limited ability to digest large amounts; low-carb diets are preferred

**Moisture content matters**: cats evolved in arid environments and have a low thirst drive. Wet food (70–80% moisture) supports urinary tract health. Dry-only diets are linked to increased UTI and kidney disease risk.

**Avoid feeding cats:**
- Onions and garlic (Heinz body anemia)
- Raw fish in large amounts (thiamine deficiency)
- Dog food as a primary diet (insufficient taurine and protein)
- Dairy products — most adult cats are lactose-intolerant
- Grapes and raisins
- Xylitol
- Alcohol, caffeine

## Feeding Frequency by Life Stage

| Life Stage | Dog | Cat |
|---|---|---|
| Newborn–4 weeks | Nursing / bottle feed every 2 hrs | Nursing / bottle feed every 2 hrs |
| 4–8 weeks | 4 meals/day | 4 meals/day |
| 2–6 months | 3 meals/day | 3–4 meals/day |
| 6–12 months | 2–3 meals/day | 2–3 meals/day |
| Adult (1–7 yrs) | 2 meals/day | 2 meals/day |
| Senior (7+ yrs) | 2 smaller meals/day | 2–3 smaller meals/day |

## Water Requirements

Fresh water must always be available. Daily water intake guidelines:

- Dogs: approximately 1 ounce (30 mL) per pound of body weight per day.
- Cats: approximately 3.5–4.5 ounces (100–135 mL) per day; more if eating dry food.

Signs of dehydration: lethargy, dry/tacky gums, sunken eyes, skin that doesn't spring back quickly when gently pinched. Dehydration is a medical emergency — seek veterinary care promptly.

## Weight Management

**Ideal body condition**: ribs easily felt but not visible; waist visible from above; abdomen tucked when viewed from the side.

If your pet is overweight:
- Reduce daily portion by 10–15% and reassess in 2 weeks.
- Switch from free-choice feeding to scheduled meals.
- Increase exercise gradually.
- Use low-calorie treats (carrot pieces for dogs, small freeze-dried protein for cats).
- Consult your veterinarian before starting any weight-loss plan, especially for cats — rapid weight loss can cause hepatic lipidosis (fatty liver disease).

## Supplements

Most pets on complete, balanced commercial diets do not require supplements. Over-supplementation can cause toxicity (especially fat-soluble vitamins A and D). Commonly recommended supplements when veterinary-indicated:

- **Omega-3 fatty acids (fish oil)**: anti-inflammatory; supports skin, coat, and joint health.
- **Probiotics**: gut health support after antibiotics or gastrointestinal illness.
- **Glucosamine/chondroitin**: joint support for senior or large-breed dogs.
- **Taurine**: for cats on homemade diets or certain grain-free foods linked to dilated cardiomyopathy.

Always consult a veterinarian before adding supplements to your pet's diet.
