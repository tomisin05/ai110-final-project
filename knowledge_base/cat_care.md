# Cat Care Guide

## Feeding Guidelines

Adult cats should be fed twice daily — morning (07:00–08:00) and evening (18:00–19:00). Cats are obligate carnivores and require high-protein diets. Avoid free-feeding dry kibble exclusively, as it contributes to obesity and urinary tract issues.

Wet food is beneficial for hydration since cats have a naturally low thirst drive. Mix wet and dry food or offer wet food at least once daily. Fresh water should be available at all times; many cats prefer running water (consider a pet fountain).

Kittens under 1 year need 3–4 small meals daily for healthy growth.

## Litter Box Maintenance

The standard rule is one litter box per cat, plus one extra. Scoop the litter box at least once daily — cats are fastidious and may refuse a dirty box, leading to inappropriate elimination. Full litter replacement should occur every 1–2 weeks for clumping litter.

Place litter boxes in quiet, accessible locations away from food and water bowls. Covered boxes appeal to privacy-seeking cats but can trap odors; check regularly.

## Grooming

- Short-haired cats: brush once per week to remove loose fur and prevent hairballs.
- Long-haired cats (Maine Coon, Persian): brush daily to prevent mats and tangles.
- Nail trimming: every 2–3 weeks. Use cat-specific nail clippers and avoid the quick (pink area).
- Dental care: brush teeth 2–3 times per week with cat-safe toothpaste. Dental treats and toys can supplement brushing.
- Cats generally self-groom effectively; baths are rarely needed unless medically indicated.

## Enrichment and Play

Cats are natural hunters and need daily play to prevent boredom and obesity. Schedule at least two play sessions daily (10–15 minutes each), ideally morning and evening:

- Wand/feather toys simulate prey and satisfy hunting instincts.
- Puzzle feeders slow eating and provide mental stimulation.
- Climbing structures and window perches encourage natural behavior and vertical territory.
- Rotate toys regularly to maintain novelty.

Indoor cats particularly need enrichment because they lack the natural stimulation of an outdoor environment.

## Health Monitoring

Monitor weight monthly — obesity affects over 50% of pet cats and leads to diabetes, arthritis, and liver disease. A healthy cat's ribs should be palpable but not visible.

Watch for behavioral changes: hiding, changes in appetite, altered litter box habits, or increased vocalization are early signs of illness in cats, who instinctively mask pain.

Annual veterinary exams are recommended; senior cats (10+ years) benefit from bi-annual checkups. Dental exams, bloodwork, and parasite prevention should be discussed with your vet.

## Common Health Concerns

- **Urinary tract disease (FLUTD)**: More common in male cats and those fed dry food only. Signs include straining to urinate, blood in urine, or crying at the litter box. Seek veterinary care immediately if your cat cannot urinate.
- **Obesity**: Increases risk of diabetes, joint disease, and liver lipidosis. Feed measured portions; avoid free-choice dry feeding.
- **Hyperthyroidism**: Common in cats over 10 years. Symptoms include weight loss despite increased appetite, hyperactivity, and vomiting.
- **Dental disease**: Affects most cats over age 3. Regular dental care reduces systemic disease risk.
- **Hairballs**: Long-haired cats and frequent groomers are more susceptible. Regular brushing and hairball-formula food or treats can help.
